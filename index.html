The subject of this talk

The ESDN: Environmental Sensing and Data Network

A shared wireless network that brings environmental measurements from the field to researchers and communities in near real time.

  • Sites across eastern North Carolina, from the ECU campus to the Outer Banks, built entirely from off-the-shelf parts
  • Brings old and new sensors from different manufacturers into one shared system; other software can read the data directly through open data connections (APIs)
  • Follows fast water processes: saltwater intrusion into farmland, groundwater and surface water exchange, urban stormwater
  • Designed for adoption, not as a black box: partners either use a turnkey deployment with full access to their data, or replicate the architecture with our support. Aim: broaden access to low-cost, real-time observation.

The science question

Coastal plain processes are fast and coupled

The Critical Zone is the layer from the treetops down to groundwater, where air, soil and water interact. On the coastal plain these interactions are rapid and frequently saline:

  • Seawater moves over land with storm surge, under land through groundwater, and both ways through channels (Helton et al. 2025)
  • A single intrusion episode can last a few hours or many months (Manda et al. 2014)
  • Urban stormwater on campus responds on the timescale of a single storm event

Periodic sampling and occasional logger downloads cannot resolve processes at the timescales that govern them.

[~1.5 min]

  • Critical Zone: treetops to groundwater, where atmosphere, soil, surface water and groundwater interact
  • Photos: Hyde County fields a few feet above sea level; the drainage canals that also carry salt water inland
  • CLICK three pathways for seawater: overland (storm surge), subsurface (groundwater), channels (both directions)
  • CLICK an intrusion episode lasts hours to months
  • CLICK campus stormwater responds within one storm event
  • Key point: visits every few weeks undersample these processes → the scientific case for continuous, real-time data

Context

How field data has been collected

1

Manual sampling

A person visits the site, measures or collects samples, and analyzes them in the lab.

Strength direct, high-accuracy lab analyses
Limitation episodic; short events fall between visits
2

Standalone data loggers

A battery-powered box records sensor readings continuously; data is downloaded during site visits.

Strength continuous, low-power, mature and simple
Limitation data and failures are only seen at the next visit
3

Commercial telemetry

A logger with a cellular modem sends data to the vendor's cloud platform.

Strength near real-time, turnkey, vendor-supported
Limitation recurring per-device fees; data and dashboards specific to each vendor
4

Open IoT networks

Standard radios and open-source software connect sensors from any manufacturer to one shared platform.

Strength near real-time, no per-device fee, interoperable
Limitation requires in-house expertise and time to operate

[~1.5 min]

  • Neutral overview: each method is still appropriate for some purposes
  • 1 · Manual sampling: lab-grade chemistry; blind between visits
  • 2 · Standalone loggers: the field-science workhorse; failures (e.g. a dead battery) are seen only at the next visit
  • 3 · Commercial telemetry: near real-time; per-device fees; data held in each vendor's system (the padlock)
  • 4 · Open IoT networks: standard radios + open-source software, any manufacturer, one platform
  • The cost of option 4 is expertise and staff time, not subscriptions; we will be explicit about that

If asked

  • "IoT" = Internet of Things: devices connected to the internet

Sensing challenge · 2 of 3 · Data quality

Data that keeps coming, but cannot be trusted

A sensor can report on schedule while its values drift, spike or flatline. Without independent reference checks, the error is undetectable.

Motivation

Limitations that led to the ESDN

Recurring costsPer-logger cellular telemetry and cloud subscriptions, about $200 per logger per year in our cost model.
Vendor lock-inEach brand comes with its own logger and its own cloud. Several brands means several clouds, with data and dashboards scattered across them.
Fragmented dataAPIs can pull it all back into one place, but that means custom code for every vendor. Why not run the whole pipeline ourselves?
Delayed fault detectionFailures found at the next site visit; gaps reconstructed after the fact.
Knowledge turnoverStation know-how leaves with rotating students and staff.
Design requirements
Most importantly: do not reinvent the wheel
  • Keep the sensors we already own
  • One shared platform for every site
  • Fully customizable, site by site
  • Turnkey access for partners, in their own workspace
  • Automatic alerts when something fails
  • Open, standard APIs and no per-device fees

The key idea

What gives a sensor an "IoT" label is connectivity, not a breakthrough in measurement.

Your METER sensor from 2019 is an IoT sensor. It just does not know it yet.

That is what the ESDN does: keep the existing sensor, add a radio, and it becomes a connected device.

Industrial, Scientific and Medical bands can be used without an individual licence, as long as devices follow power and airtime rules (in the US, FCC Part 15).

Shared, not emptyMany technologies use the same bands; rules limit how loud and how long each device transmits.

Part 2 · The radio · Gateways

Gateways: siting and installation

  • What sets coverage: antenna height, terrain and landscape (buildings, trees, open water), antenna quality, and node configuration. Landscape matters more than the hardware specification.
  • Indoor gateway: plugs into a wall socket and the building network; set up in minutes, but covers a building or a small campus area. Useful to fill gaps.
  • Outdoor gateway: can be a major effort. A high site, the owner's permission and access, power (outlet, network cable or solar), an internet connection (Ethernet, LTE or Starlink), and weatherproof mounting.
  • No tall structure available: a low-cost 30 ft flagpole, installed by one person.

[~1.5 min]

  • Analogy: a gateway is a mailbox: it does not read the letters, it just forwards them

  • What sets coverage: height, terrain, buildings, vegetation, water, antenna quality, node configuration

  • Outdoor: km-scale range, but a major effort: high site, owner permission, power, backhaul (Ethernet, LTE, Starlink), weatherproofing

Part 2 · The radio · Essential concept

Overlapping gateways: one message, one copy, many apps

OverlapA node is not paired with a gateway. Every gateway in range forwards what it hears.
DeduplicateThe network server keeps one copy, and uses the best gateway to reply.
IntegrateThe application server stores the reading and hands it to other apps through standard web connections.

[~1.5 min]

  • Analogy: three witnesses report the same event; the clerk files it once and notes who saw it best
  • Nodes are not paired with a gateway; they just transmit
  • Every gateway in range forwards its own copy (here, three); gateway 4 is on the same network but too far from this node, so it hears nothing
  • Network server (TTN or ChirpStack): deduplicates, keeps one, remembers the best gateway for replies
  • Application server (ThingsBoard): stores, then distributes to dashboards, alerts, open data, partner apps
  • Every other loop one gateway goes offline; the message still arrives
  • Key point: overlap is redundancy, not waste; it fills the coverage gaps on the map

Lessons learned

Eight lessons from two years of operation

Start under $200A $60 indoor gateway, a $70 sensor, free TTN and ThingsBoard Cloud. Trust the pipeline before you self-host.
Retrofit, do not replaceSDI-12 compliance makes it almost trivial. Keep the long record intact.
Alerts change fieldwork15-minute failure detection means you arrive with the right spare, not a guess.
Spatial coverage over consolidationWithout per-device fees, sensor placement can follow the science rather than the billing.
Landscape over datasheetsMap coverage empirically; add gateways opportunistically; overlap them.
Validate cheap sensorsLab and longitudinal field checks. Budget the time.
Metadata is infrastructureCalibration logs, deployment histories and QA/QC flags live inside the system, from day one.
Networks grow heterogeneouslySites differ in maturity and design. Record those differences as metadata, not in someone's notebook.

[~1.5 min · one sentence per card]

  • Start under $200 · Retrofit, do not replace · Alerts change fieldwork
  • Spatial coverage over consolidation · Landscape over datasheets (map coverage; overlap gateways)
  • Validate low-cost sensors; budget the time
  • Metadata is infrastructure: calibration, deployment history, QC flags in the system from day one
  • Networks grow heterogeneously: record site differences as metadata

Backup · Network ownership

Network ownership: user-managed, third-party or hybrid

Topology and ownership are the same decision.

User-managed

LoRaWAN, Wi-Fi, mesh. You place the gateways, you know the coverage, the backhaul is yours. No per-device fee.

Third-party

Cellular, satellite. Immediate coverage and no hardware to install; coverage depends on the provider, with a subscription per device.

Hybrid

Start on cellular, then move nodes onto your own gateways as coverage grows. The path the ESDN followed.
